\title{Balade: How Little Arrows Learned to Feel Alive}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Fox3000foxy}\IEEEauthorblockA{Independent Researcher\\fox3000foxy@users.noreply.github.com}}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Balade is a browser extension that places small autonomous cursors on web
pages. They stroll, look at things, type ghost words, greet each other, and
sometimes sulk. No neural network is involved: everything is classical
mathematics plus small state machines. This paper explains, with pictures,
how four ingredients make the difference between a robot and a hand.
\end{abstract}

\section{Useless---and proud of it}
Balade saves you zero seconds. It does not organize anything or measure
anything. It simply makes little arrows live on your pages. That is the
whole point: a playground for asking how close mathematics can get to a
human gesture, and the answer turns out to be: surprisingly close.

\section{Four ingredients, everywhere}
No animation in Balade is linear, constant, or synchronized. Every gesture
combines a curve, an easing, a duration that is not proportional to distance,
and a little bounded randomness. Fig.~\ref{fig:arc} shows the first one: a
real hand draws arcs, never straight lines.

\section{How long does it take? Fitts's law}
Reaching a target takes longer when it is far, but shorter when it is big:
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{0.08 + 0.085\,\log_2(1 + D/W)}{v}
\end{equation}
A target twice as far takes much less than twice the time: the hand makes a
fast ballistic throw, then slows down to aim. Fig.~\ref{fig:fitts} shows the
shape: steep at first, flattening out.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering

\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Fitts's law: doubling the distance never doubles the time.}
\label{fig:fitts}
\end{figure}

\section{The shape of speed: minimum jerk}
A human hand minimizes sudden jolts. The position profile is the famous
minimum-jerk polynomial, warped in time so the speed peak lands before the
middle: a sharp start, a long gentle landing (Fig.~\ref{fig:jerk}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}

\section{Settling down: the spring}
Once near the target, the cursor behaves like a mass on a spring with damping:
\begin{equation}
a = -k\,x - c\,v
\end{equation}
Integrated with semi-implicit Euler (velocity first, then position), it can
never explode: the cursor always settles.

\section{Flying together: the swarm}
Held Shift gathers every cursor around your pointer with Reynolds steering:
an orbit that breathes ($\pm18\%$), a 3-lobe rosace, and a swirl that flips
direction about every 29~s. Fig.~\ref{fig:rosace} shows the ring the eye
never quite catches as a perfect circle.

\section{Small brains: habits, moods, manners}
Cursors learn click sequences per site (first-order Markov chains, all
local). They get bored: satiety grows with visits and decays over ten
minutes, so a much-visited element becomes boring and unseen ones attract.
They have roles (leader, follower, loner) and moods (chill, grumpy, playful,
proud). A loner that bumps into a typing cursor gets a ``hey, my spot!''
and shoves it gently aside. Nothing is sent to any page: the ghost text is
an overlay, the field value never changes.

\section{Fast, and measured}
Every optimization was benchmarked. Iterative heading rotation ($4\times$,
bit-exact), sampled progress curves (zero \texttt{pow} per frame), the
triple-angle expansion without \texttt{atan2} ($3\times$), squared distances
($2\times$). Rejected after measurement: a sine table ($3\times$ slower),
Taylor order 5 (wrong), a cosine pre-filter (slower than \texttt{acos}).
Details in the repository README.

\section{Conclusion}
Four ingredients, classical formulas, small state machines---and arrows that
feel held by someone. No learning required.
